# Compositing & Matching

> Optional craft notes for placing a supplied or generated element into filmed footage when the result should feel like it belongs in the shot. A deliberately graphic, surreal, or visibly inserted treatment may be better when realism is not the goal.

## Make the relationship believable

Start from the shot and the element's role. Then ask the few questions that determine whether they belong together:

- **Viewpoint and scale:** Does the element share the shot's camera angle, perspective, horizon, and believable size? A correct cutout can still float if its geometry belongs to another view.
- **Contact and occlusion:** Where does it touch, rest, cast a shadow, or pass behind something? Let foreground objects cover it when they should, and let a hand, surface, or edge visibly affect the relationship.
- **Light and reflection:** Do the direction, softness, color, and intensity of light agree? Add or preserve a contact shadow, reflected color, or surface response only when the shot supports it.
- **Motion:** Does it track with the camera, subject, and surface? Match the pace, motion blur, focus changes, and any small movement that makes the element feel attached to the scene.
- **Image character:** Does it share the shot's sharpness, depth of field, grain, noise, compression, and color? An element that is too clean or too sharp can look less believable than one with less detail.

These are ways to diagnose a mismatch, not a mandatory checklist or a promise that a model will solve it automatically. A locked camera, simple background, or clear foreground separation can make the job easier than a technically ambitious interaction.

## Borrow a simpler treatment when needed

If an element cannot plausibly touch or move through the scene, change the relationship instead of spending effort disguising the failure. Keep it on a stable surface, place it in a clean insert or picture-in-picture, use a deliberate graphic treatment, show it before or after the live-action shot, or choose a new angle with fewer occlusion and tracking demands. A clear, intentionally composited callout often communicates more than a distracting attempt at invisible realism.

Use the supplied footage, references, and intended claim as the source of truth. A generated object can illustrate a concept, but it does not establish that a real product performed a test. When realism or factual identity matters, inspect the finished shot for geometry, label or face fidelity, contact, temporal stability, and whether the composite changes what the footage appears to prove.
