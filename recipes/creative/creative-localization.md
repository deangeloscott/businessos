# Creative Localization

> Optional craft reference for adapting images, video, audio, presentations, or related creative work to another language or market. Use the depth the job needs; translating a caption does not require recreating an entire production. The active model and tools choose how to translate, subtitle, dub, edit, or rebuild the relevant parts.

## Preserve the intended meaning and effect

Resolve the intended language, locale, audience, and use from the request and existing context. Reuse established terminology, names, pronunciation, source claims, and organization preferences. Ask only when an unresolved choice materially affects the result.

Decide what should remain exact and what can be adapted: factual claims and offer terms need faithful meaning, while idioms, jokes, examples, rhythm, and imagery may need a different expression to have the intended effect. Preserve a speaker's point of view without forcing source-language word order. Check cultural references rather than assuming a familiar symbol or joke transfers. [TED's translation guidance](https://www.ted.com/participate/translate/guidelines) offers examples of preserving tone and handling idioms; TED's house style is not a requirement for other work.

Do not silently change a price, service promise, availability, testimonial, or product demonstration to make it fit a market. Use verified local information or retain the uncertainty. A natural-sounding translation does not establish that a market-specific claim is true.

## Adapt the channels that carry the meaning

Choose subtitles, dubbing, changed graphics, a new performance, a recut, or a smaller combination according to the audience and available source. Check meaningful on-screen labels, screenshots, diagrams, units, dates, and calls to action as well as spoken words. Preserve actual quantities when converting units; use locally appropriate notation where it helps understanding.

Translated text can require different space, fonts, line breaks, or reading direction. Reflow the design rather than shrinking important text until it is unreadable. Keep text editable when further adaptation is likely. [W3C's text-size guidance](https://www.w3.org/International/articles/article-text-size) explains why translated length and layout need attention; no fixed expansion ratio fits every language or phrase.

For speech, balance natural delivery, pronunciation, intended emotion, and available time. Rephrase or adjust the edit when useful; forcing every translation into the original timing can damage meaning or performance. Preserve useful background sound and music, and recheck sync and audibility after changes. If likeness or voice identity matters, draw on the [continuity reference](video/notes/identity-and-continuity.md).

## Review the adapted artifact

Inspect or listen to the actual target-language result for meaning, omissions, terminology, performance, text rendering, and timing. Check subtitles against the final edit. Translated dialogue alone may omit speaker identification or important non-speech sound needed for accessibility; use the appropriate caption treatment. [W3C's captions/subtitles guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/) explains this distinction.

For example, a sandwich-making tutorial may need new narration, ingredient labels, and subtitle timing while preserving the demonstrated preparation. A joke may need a different setup, but the shop's actual ingredients do not change with the translation.

If reliable target-language review is unavailable, describe that coverage limit and use suitable review when it would materially change confidence. A reverse translation or source-language review alone cannot establish natural target-language delivery. Preserve useful adapted versions, editable sources, and recurring terminology or pronunciation choices through existing Assets and preferences. Current tool documentation supplies implementation details; no provider-specific dubbing procedure is required here.
