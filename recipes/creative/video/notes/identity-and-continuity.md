# Creative Identity & Continuity

> Optional craft guidance. Use this when a person, avatar, voice, character, or environment needs to remain recognizable across assets or be deliberately changed while keeping its identity. It is a reference, not a required dossier, prompt format, workflow graph, or provider choice.

## Start with what should survive

Decide what the audience should recognize, then preserve only those anchors. Depending on the work, that might be:

- **Appearance:** silhouette, face, hair, skin or material qualities, palette, wardrobe, or a recurring prop.
- **Voice:** vocal character, pronunciation, accent, rhythm, or sound texture. Keep voice identity distinct from delivery energy and from the words or communication style.
- **Performance:** gaze, posture, gestures, expressions, timing, or a characteristic way of handling an object.
- **Communication:** vocabulary, directness, humor, formality, point of view, or recurring ways of explaining something.
- **Environment:** layout, relative landmarks, spatial anchors, materials, objects, or other features that make a place recognizable.

Choose only the dimensions that matter. Keep intentional variation separate from identity: a person can change clothes, energy, or setting; a room can change time of day; a mascot can take different actions without becoming a different mascot. A compact direction such as **keep / change / avoid** is often more useful than an exhaustive character bible.

One clear reference may be enough. Add another image, angle, speaking clip, performance, writing sample, or environment view only when it answers a real ambiguity or improves a repeated result. Use the strongest supplied source rather than asking the user to complete a form the task does not need.

## Three useful starting situations

### Create something original

Give the new identity a small set of memorable anchors and a range in which it can move. For an original mascot, that could be a distinct silhouette, two-color palette, curious head movement, and warm concise voice. Let outfits, props, expressions, and settings vary when they serve the scene. The anchor is the recognizable combination, not a frozen pose.

For a recurring environment, preserve the floor plan, relative landmarks, and a few objects or materials that tell the viewer where they are. The camera viewpoint, weather, time, and lighting can change without losing the place.

### Derive it from a supplied or real reference

Name what must remain recognizable and what should become new. A digital presenter might preserve a person's face, vocal character, and natural gesture pattern while using a new script, setting, wardrobe, and slightly more energetic delivery. A reference-derived environment might preserve its layout and window relationship while changing the season or lighting.

Use source material and likeness, voice, or style choices only within the permissions and disclosure requirements that apply to the actual work. The reference should inform the requested result; it does not by itself establish permission, authenticity, expertise, or a performance claim.

### Improve something that already exists

Compare the current result with the intended identity, identify the specific drift, and change only what needs changing. If an avatar still looks right but has become expressionless, improve gaze and gesture while preserving its face and silhouette. If the voice is recognizable but flat, adjust pacing and emphasis rather than replacing the voice. If a recurring room has drifted, restore its spatial anchors before redesigning its mood.

Preserve what already works. Rebuilding every component can introduce more variation than the original problem.

## Choose the least complicated production path

An integrated tool may handle appearance, voice, script, and performance together. A modular approach may use separate image, voice, performance, compositing, and editing capabilities when that gives better control. Existing footage or a real voice recording may be the strongest starting point when generating a replacement would reduce authenticity or add unnecessary work.

These are available approaches, not phases. The model or harness can choose and coordinate only the capabilities the result needs, or use a better method it already knows. AURA contributes relevant context and reusable Asset references; the active tools handle generation, editing, rendering, and application-specific project files.

For example, **“Create a digital presenter based on me. Preserve my recognizable appearance, voice, and mannerisms, but make the delivery a little more energetic”** may use a single integrated avatar tool, a real recording with an editorial pass, or separate visual, speech, and performance tools. The useful direction is the identity target and the allowed change, not a mandated sequence.

## Retain only useful continuity

When future work will reuse the identity, preserve the selected reference files or provider references, useful source lineage, version, and a short keep/change direction through the existing Asset and memory primitives. Keep the real files in a durable location. Do not ingest every raw recording, rejected generation, temporary prompt, or intermediate take, and do not create a new identity object or registry just to organize them.

Inspect the result for the anchors that matter: recognizable appearance, voice character and pronunciation, intended performance changes, communication style, environment geometry, and continuity between shots or assets. For an existing identity repair, state the reference, name the drift, preserve the successful parts, and correct the smallest set of features that caused the problem. Compare the revision in the actual intended crop, playback, or listening context. Correct drift that affects the viewer's understanding; allow variation that was intentional. Existing avatar, audio, visual-direction, accessibility, rights, and editorial guidance remains available when the job needs it.
