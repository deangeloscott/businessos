# Trend or distribution graphic

Use this when the audience needs to see how something changes over time, varies across a group, or relates to another measure.

## The idea

Choose the visual form from the question. A line can show a meaningful sequence, bars can compare categories, a dot or range can show spread, and a small table can be clearer than a chart when exact values matter. Make the comparison, unit, time window, and source easy to find before adding style.

## Borrowable shape

**Question → focused chart or table → direct labels and units → short takeaway → source, scope, and uncertainty.**

Show only the series or categories that answer the question. Use a consistent scale and clear baseline for comparisons, avoid decorative 3D or unexplained color, and provide labels or a text alternative when the graphic must work without visual perception. If the data cannot support a trend or relationship, show the observations without implying causation.

## Tiny illustrative example

**How did requests change across the quarter?**

A line chart shows requests by week, with a note identifying the reporting period and source. The caption says **“The pattern rises late in the period; investigate the cause before treating it as a forecast.”** Use actual observations and preserve the uncertainty.

## Adapt

Let the audience's question determine the chart, not the chart type you happen to have. Include a denominator when a rate could be mistaken for a count, annotate a meaningful event when the source supports one, and keep missing or estimated values visible. Use the executive brief or one-number recipe when the reader needs a conclusion more than a full distribution.
