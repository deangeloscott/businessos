# Conceptual and editorial image

> Optional craft reference. Use this when an image should create an association, mood, question, joke, or sense of character rather than document a literal scene. It can support education, editorial work, entertainment, culture, or a brand; it does not need to sell or resolve the idea for the viewer.

## Choose the relationship

- **Metaphor:** let another object, place, or action carry the idea.
- **Juxtaposition:** place unlike things together so their relationship creates tension or humor.
- **Mood:** use light, space, color, texture, weather, or stillness to make a feeling tangible.
- **Character:** let a person, creature, or object reveal a point of view through posture, gesture, costume, or setting.
- **Ambiguity:** withhold one piece of the explanation when curiosity or interpretation is part of the work.

These are expressive choices, not a formula. A literal image, an abstract one, a strange image, or a quiet one may be the better answer.

## Borrowable shape

**Idea or feeling → visual relationship → world, character, or moment → crop or series role.**

For a story about being overwhelmed, a calm desk beneath a ceiling of softly drifting notifications may carry the feeling without spelling it out. For a playful editorial piece, a tiny suited figure negotiating with an enormous household object can let scale carry the joke. Remove symbols that explain the image twice; keep the unresolved space when the audience is meant to wonder.

## Adapt to the work

Decide how literal, staged, surreal, documentary, or illustrative the image should be. Let the subject, light, palette, texture, framing, and expression support the chosen relationship instead of adding mood adjectives without a visual consequence. If the image is part of a series, keep a useful motif, character, or visual rule while allowing each frame to have its own situation.

Design the key relationship to survive the actual crop and viewing size. Keep text, captions, and logos separate and editable when revision or localization is likely; baked text can be faster for a fixed composition but is harder to correct or translate. If a conceptual image could be mistaken for documentation, make its illustrative or composite status clear and do not use it as evidence of an event, person, product, or result.

Judge it by the feeling, question, or character it leaves with the viewer—not by whether it forces one universal takeaway.
