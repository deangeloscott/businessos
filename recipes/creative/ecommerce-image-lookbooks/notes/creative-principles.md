# Image Creative Principles

> Optional craft notes. Open this when a selected image idea needs more reasoning. A model or user with a clear direction can use the recipe directly, combine it with another approach, or skip the library entirely.

## Start with the viewer's question

Choose the image for the job it needs to do, not because a product happens to belong to a category. Common jobs are:

- **Make the product legible:** form, contents, scale, fit, included parts, or finish.
- **Make a difference visible:** a mechanism, material, use state, or generic poor-versus-premium contrast.
- **Make the product desirable in context:** a habitat, ritual, or aspirational setting.
- **Make use feel tangible:** touch, pouring, application, movement, texture, or a satisfying actuation.

The six scale menus make physical references easier to find. They are browsing aids, not a sequence. The four jobs are useful labels, not a taxonomy that every asset must obey. A strong image may combine them, and another structure may be better for the brief.

## Five portable decisions

### 1. Give the viewer one dominant thing to understand

Lead with the product, the action, or the contrast that carries the idea. If the frame needs several facts, separate them with panels, labels, a sequence, or additional images. A single busy scene rarely explains everything well.

### 2. Borrow a physical cue, not a stock adjective

“Premium” is weak direction by itself. A raking highlight can reveal a machined edge; a contact shadow can ground a heavy object; a familiar hand, doorway, cabinet, or vehicle can establish scale; a controlled pour can make texture easier to imagine. Choose the cue that helps the viewer answer the question.

Camera position determines perspective; focal length helps choose framing from that position. Use exact lens numbers only when they help the chosen tool or production, not as universal laws.

### 3. Let hierarchy do the work

Use size, focus, luminance, color, depth, negative space, and prop restraint to make the intended subject obvious. Occupancy percentages, f-stops, color temperatures, and contrast ratios in the recipes are useful starting choices for that execution. Change them when the crop, material, accessibility, brand, or communication job calls for it.

### 4. Use contrast deliberately

A generic weak state beside a strong hero state can make value and relief immediate. It may be intentionally stark and persuasive. Keep the alternative generic unless a named competitor and its attributes are actually supplied. Keep the hero's materials, results, measurements, and performance language grounded in the available source. A generated illustration can dramatize a mechanism or desired experience; it does not become a test record by looking convincing.

Pain-to-relief, premium-versus-poor, and problem-to-solution framing are choices the user or model can make when they fit the audience. Reverse the usual left-to-right arrangement when reading direction, culture, placement, or composition benefits from it.

### 5. Inspect what the prompt cannot guarantee

Check the final asset at its intended size and crop. Look for warped type, wrong components, extra limbs, impossible reflections, floating products, broken contact, false dimensions, implausible material behavior, and a contrast that implies more than the source supports. Reference images, masks, vectors, real footage, 3D, and post-render compositing may be better than adding more prompt text when fidelity matters.

## Persuasion choices

The model can reason about white-hat, grey-hat, and black-hat approaches when choosing how forceful a concept should be:

- **White-hat:** direct, transparent, evidence-led communication.
- **Grey-hat:** selective emphasis, strong comparison, urgency, and emotional pain framing whose underlying statements remain supportable.
- **Black-hat:** deceptive pressure such as fabricated scarcity, invented results, impersonation, hidden terms, or dark-pattern concealment.

These labels describe tactics and tradeoffs; they do not turn a recipe into a permission layer or runtime guard. The useful distinction is whether a scene is a supplied fact, an observed result, a hypothesis, or an illustration. The user and model can choose the intensity and method that fit the actual job, while keeping unknowns from being presented as known.

## Optional lenses for choosing a starting point

Awareness and sophistication can help when the audience is known: an unfamiliar viewer may need a striking habitat or ritual; a problem-aware viewer may need a relief contrast; a product-aware viewer may need fit, dimensions, mechanism, or finish. These are hypotheses about what may help, not fixed mappings from audience stage to recipe. Use customer language, the brief, and the placement to decide.

A brand dialect is another optional lens. Borrow a few lighting, material, palette, or cultural cues from [DIALECTS.md](../DIALECTS.md) when they sharpen the idea. Keep product legibility, accessibility, platform requirements, and truthful claims above a mood reference. Combining dialects or inventing a new treatment is allowed when it serves the brand.

## A quick adaptation pattern

When a starting point is useful, tell the model the product and the intended result in ordinary language: “Use the fit-at-a-glance idea for this appliance,” or “Borrow the tactile pour for this sauce.” Supply source images, dimensions, labels, material facts, or test records when they matter. The model can adapt the setting, camera, lighting, copy, and tool directly, produce a tailored prompt, or recommend a different approach. Asking a long placeholder questionnaire is rarely necessary unless a missing fact changes the answer.

Keep the smallest useful amount of this note in context. The individual recipe should be enough to begin; these principles are here when a choice needs explanation or when several recipes need to be combined.
