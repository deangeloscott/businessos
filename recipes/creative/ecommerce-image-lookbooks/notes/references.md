## Selected craft references

Use the source that answers the current question instead of loading a bibliography into every task.

### Light, camera, and material

- Fil Hunter, Steven Biver, and Paul Fuqua, *Light: Science & Magic* — practical reflection, diffusion, and lighting for objects.
- Matt Pharr, Wenzel Jakob, and Greg Humphreys, *Physically Based Rendering* — light transport and material rendering reference: <https://www.pbr-book.org/>.
- The camera or image tool's current documentation — controls and behavior change by tool and version.

These references support modest craft cues: a highlight can reveal curvature, a contact shadow can ground an object, and camera distance changes perspective. They do not justify fixed lens, token, occupancy, or prompt rules.

### Viewer understanding and persuasion

- John Sweller, “Cognitive Load During Problem Solving” (*Cognitive Science*, 1988) — a useful reason to keep the viewer's main job clear; it does not prove a fixed layout.
- Joann Peck and Terry Childers, work on need for touch in consumer decisions — a reason to consider scale and texture; it does not predict every audience response.
- Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* — an optional lens for awareness and message sophistication; use audience-specific judgment.

### Product imagery and usability

- Baymard Institute's current product-page research — useful questions about gallery order, scale, and shopping friction; verify current guidance at the source.
- The organization's own product references, CAD, tech packs, samples, tests, packaging files, and platform requirements — the appropriate sources for product facts.

Keep source claims narrow. If a recipe depicts a result, the product record, source image, measured test, or other supplied evidence must carry that claim. A polished render can illustrate a mechanism or desired experience without establishing it.
